Grille snorkels, a duct and a sealed airbox for the N54

My silver N54 135i breathes through two printed snorkels behind the kidney grille, a duct across the radiator support and a sealed airbox around its two cone filters. I ran the whole intake through CFD at the airflow for about 550 whp to see where the pressure goes before the turbos, then redesigned the duct, lowered the lid for the hood, and checked what an open lid and a hot engine bay would cost.

The parts

Three printed pieces take the air from behind the grille to the turbo inlet pipes. Each is an STL on Cults3D.

  • Grille snorkels. Two boxes that bolt behind the kidney grille openings and pick the air up right there, ahead of the radiator. My red LS-swapped 135i runs the same pair into its own intake. Listed as the E82 air intake duct.
  • Relocated inlet duct. Runs across the radiator support from the snorkels to the airbox. The E82 135i relocated inlet air duct.
  • Sealed airbox. A box and a screw-down lid around the two cone filters on the turbo inlet pipes, so the turbos only see the ducted air. TPU gaskets seal the duct into its socket and the floor ports around the pipes. The E82 N54 relocated inlet airbox.

What the CFD says

At 0.48 kg/s, half to each turbo, starting from still air behind the grille, with each filter assumed to drop 500 Pa:

Front turbo pipeRear turbo pipe
Total pressure lost12.96 kPa12.52 kPa
Vacuum in the pipe18.6 kPa18.9 kPa

About 12.7 kPa (1.85 psi) of total pressure is gone before the turbos. The vacuum in the pipes is bigger, about 18.8 kPa, because it also includes the speed of the air itself: 82 m/s in a 55.6 mm pipe is about 4 kPa that isn't lost, just moving. The compressors start from about 89 kPa absolute instead of 101, so for the same boost each turbo works at a pressure ratio about 14% higher.

The two snorkels don't share the work. The passenger one, next to where the duct turns into the box, takes 71% of the air. The driver one's air has to cross the car through the duct's narrowest stretch, about 2,300 mm² at close to 50 m/s, so it takes 29%.

The big one is the duct's round end. It is about 74 mm across inside, sitting in a 94 mm socket, and all the air leaves it at about 93 m/s, up to about 170 m/s on the inside of its last bend. Nothing in the box slows that jet down gently, so its speed, 5 to 6.5 kPa worth, is simply lost. The front filter sits in the jet's path and soaks up a lot of it: 4.2 kPa goes in its media against 1.0 kPa in the rear filter. Either way it's gone before the turbos.

The redesigned duct

So I redesigned the duct. The round end now matches the airbox socket, and the duct stays wide from the middle of the car all the way out to the passenger side.

  • The round end. 100 mm outside and 94 mm bore, the same as the airbox socket, so the bore runs straight on into the box. It butts against the socket rim, and a printed coupler slides over the joint: 50 mm long, 25 mm on each side, with a 0.4 mm slip clearance I can change after I rescan the neck.
  • The back of the duct. It sits about 20 mm further back from the middle of the car out to the passenger end, then turns into the round end in one smooth bend. Through the middle the passage is 34 to 46% bigger.
  • What stays. The front wall and both snorkel sockets are unchanged, so the snorkels bolt on as before.

Same CFD as above, same 0.48 kg/s, same assumed filters, both on the coarse mesh (on today's duct the coarse mesh came within 0.5% of the finer one):

Duct todayRedesigned duct
Total pressure lost before the turbos12.8 kPa9.7 kPa
Vacuum in the turbo pipes18.7 kPa16.1 kPa
Air leaving the duct into the box93 m/s57 m/s
Share of the air through the driver snorkel29%30%

That is 3.1 kPa less lost before the turbos, about a quarter, and 2.6 kPa less vacuum in the pipes. Most of it is the round end: the air leaves the duct at 57 m/s instead of 93, so much less of its speed is thrown away in the box.

Along the way I tried a wider middle that narrowed back to the old duct before the passenger snorkel. It came out worse than the round end on its own, 9.9 kPa against 9.4: the air slows down in the wide part and then has to speed up again. Keeping it wide all the way to the end is what made the difference.

Then the driver side

The driver snorkel's air still had to squeeze through the narrowest part of the duct, where the driver socket feeds into the crossing: about 2,440 mm² at 48 m/s. So I widened the driver side too, starting right at the socket behind the grille. The back wall moves rearward from the middle of the car across the whole driver socket, up to 38 mm, and fades back into the old wall at the driver end, where there's no room behind it. The front wall, the socket and its lugs are the same, so the driver snorkel bolts on as before.

The narrowest part goes from about 2,440 to 3,550 mm², the same as the crossing, and the chamber behind the driver snorkel is 36 to 53% bigger. Same CFD again:

Total pressure lost before the turbos9.7 kPa9.2 kPa
Vacuum in the turbo pipes16.1 kPa15.7 kPa

That is another 0.5 kPa off: 9.2 kPa in all against 12.8 for the duct on the car, 28% less. The driver snorkel now carries a third of the air instead of 30%, so the passenger snorkel and its flange work less hard, and the jet into the box is fed more evenly. From here on, "new duct" means this one.

I haven't printed the redesign yet. The files on Cults3D are still the duct as it is on the car today.

The lid and the hood

The top of the lid touched the hood. Its highest point is the dome over the rear filter, so I lowered that by 2 mm: the lid above 164 mm is the same shell pressed down, by nothing at 164 mm and by 2 mm at the old peak, so the dome just gets flatter. The wall stays 2.4 mm, the front lid screw boss comes down with it, and the rear filter's top edge still clears the underside by about 2 mm. The front filter sits under a lower part of the lid and doesn't change.

With the lid off

Taking the lid off is the baseline: nothing between the filters and the engine bay. I modelled the opening the lid covers as still air at ambient pressure, with the snorkels and duct still connected, so the air can come from either.

Vacuum in the turbo pipes18.7 kPa15.7 kPa8.6 kPa

With the lid off, 95% of the air comes straight in over the box. What's left is the filters and the entry into the turbo pipes. So the sealed box costs about 10 kPa with today's duct and 7 with the new one. On the car the hood sits on the lid, so with it off the air would have to come in through a 10 to 15 mm gap under the hood. This is the best case for an open box.

Hot air

The catch with an open lid is that the filters breathe engine-bay air, and the bay is hot. The sealed box isn't free of heat either: it sits above the turbo and heat soaks.

For the box I carried the temperature through the solved airflow with the box, lid and filter caps held hot. At full throttle the air picks up about 9% of the difference between the box and the incoming air with today's duct, 7% with the new one: a box soaked 90 °F hotter than the air warms it about 7 to 8 °F before the turbos. That is a high estimate. At part throttle the air sits in the box longer and comes out hotter, but a full-throttle pull replaces it in a few hundredths of a second.

To turn temperatures and pressure losses into power I used a simple turbo model: the tune holds 23 psi, the compressors run at 70%, the intercooler at 75% against 77 °F air, the meth takes 27 °F off the charge, and power at a held boost follows the air density in the manifold. 550 whp is the car as it is now. Those are assumptions, not measurements, and the answers are differences between setups, not dyno numbers.

The open lid only matches the sealed box if the air the filters breathe is less than about 36 °F above ambient, 24 °F against the new duct. Near the turbos it is usually hotter than that, so the sealed box wins, by about 9 whp against 140 °F bay air and 19 whp against 167 °F. The intercooler is why the gap isn't bigger: at 75% it takes out about three quarters of any change in inlet temperature before the manifold, where the N54 reads IAT. With a weaker intercooler the gap grows to 14 and 27 whp. The model leaves out timing pulled for a hotter IAT, which would make an open lid look worse still.

On track my engine bay runs well over 120 °F, and on a hot day it climbs about the same amount above the outside air. So what matters is how far the bay sits above ambient. Same model, with the sealed box heat soaked and the turbo pipes at bay temperature:

The open lid only breaks even when the bay is less than about 36 °F above ambient, 24 °F against the new duct, so with my bay well over 120 °F the sealed box stays ahead, and the hotter the bay runs the more it wins.

Wastegate duty

The tune holds boost, so the box's restriction comes back as work the turbos have to do. At the same air temperature the sealed box with today's duct needs a pressure ratio of 3.05 against 2.73 with the lid off: 13% more compressor work, 8% with the new duct. The wastegates find it by sending more exhaust through the turbines, which shows up as a higher wastegate duty cycle at the same rpm and boost. That extra work heats the air again, about 34 °F at the compressor outlet and 9 °F after the intercooler.

The cold air in the box more than pays it back. Against an open lid in 140 °F bay air, the sealed box needs only 3% more compressor work with today's duct and 1.5% less with the new one, and its charge comes out 11 °F cooler. I can't put a duty cycle percentage on it without the tune's wastegate tables; a log at the same rpm and boost with the lid on and off will show it. On the 17Ts near the top of their range the restriction also pushes the compressors about 7% further toward their flow limit, 2.5% with the new duct. If they run out of room, that shows up as boost falling off at high rpm instead.

How it was modelled

  • The parts. The snorkels, duct, airbox and lid come straight from their Fusion 360 models, placed as they sit on the car.
  • The engine bay. The cone filters and turbo inlet pipes come from 3D scans of the bay with and without the filters. Each filter is about 157 mm long, 114 mm across the base and 81 mm across the top. The pipes are 60.6 mm outside; I assumed a 2.5 mm wall.
  • Gaskets. Assumed to seal perfectly: the duct in its socket, the pipes in the floor ports, the lid seam and each snorkel's flange.
  • Airflow. 0.48 kg/s (63.5 lb/min), what an N54 needs for about 550 whp: about 10 crank hp per lb/min and 15% through the drivetrain. That is a target, not a dyno sheet.
  • Filters. Each a 12 mm porous layer on the scanned cone, set to 500 Pa at its share of the flow. That figure is assumed, not measured.
  • The solve. Steady flow in OpenFOAM, k-omega SST, about 864,000 cells, starting from still air under each snorkel. The answer moved under 1% over the last 1,000 iterations, a coarser mesh of 503,000 cells landed within 0.5% of it, and the air in and out adds up at every step from the snorkels to the box.

What this does not show

  • No road speed. The air under the snorkels is still. At 100 mph the grille sees up to about 1.2 kPa of ram pressure, which would offset part of the loss.
  • Airflow and filters are estimates. Losses go with the square of the flow: 10% more air is about 21% more loss.
  • Incompressible model. At 93 m/s the air's density changes a few percent, and more on the inside of the duct's last bend at about 170 m/s, so treat these as estimates. That bend is probably a little worse than shown.
  • Not horsepower. These are pressures in a simulated intake, not a measurement on the car. The pressure CAN node is a board I've designed to measure them; it isn't built yet. Its two 40 kPa channels cover the 19 kPa in the turbo pipes, and the 10 kPa ones suit the steps before the box.
