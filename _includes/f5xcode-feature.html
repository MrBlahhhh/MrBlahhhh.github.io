F5X Code

A diagnostics, logging, coding and service app for the third-generation MINI: the F54 Clubman, F55 five-door, F56 three-door, F57 convertible and F60 Countryman. It reads every module's fault codes in plain English, logs the engine, gearbox and steering while you drive, backs up and changes module coding, and runs the workshop jobs like battery registration and brake bleeding. What you need

An Android phone and one of two ENET connections: an ENET cable with a USB-C Ethernet adapter, or the red MHD Universal Wi-Fi adapter. ENET is the fastest link and what the Tuning presets need for the engine's full channel set. Bluetooth and Wi-Fi OBD adapters work too, more slowly.

recommendedENET cable

The OBD-to-Ethernet cable E-Sys uses, usually $15 to $25. Get one that plugs straight into the car's OBD port. A splitter usually leaves out the Ethernet pins, and then the car won't answer. Find one on Amazon.

recommendedUSB-C Ethernet adapter

Plugs into the phone, and the ENET cable plugs into it. Any USB-C to Ethernet (RJ45) adapter works; 100 Mbit is plenty. Some ENET kits come with one. Find one on Amazon.

recommendedRed MHD Universal adapter

The wireless choice, or the one to use if you already own it for MHD. It's an ENET bridge over Wi-Fi, so it's as fast as the cable and reads the same engine channels. It plugs into the OBD port and the phone joins its Wi-Fi. No USB adapter or tethering needed.

recommendedAndroid phone

Android 7 or newer. For the ENET cable, Android 11 or newer, because the cable needs Ethernet tethering, which arrived in Android 11. Look for it under Hotspot and tethering in your phone's settings.

optionalOBDLink CX or MX+

Bluetooth, no cable. The CX is Bluetooth LE and needs no pairing: choose OBDLink CX / Bluetooth LE. Pair the MX+ in Android's Bluetooth settings first, then choose Paired Bluetooth adapter.

optionalWi-Fi OBD2 adapter

A Wi-Fi ELM327-type adapter. Join its Wi-Fi, then choose Wi-Fi OBD2 adapter. The slowest link, fine for fault codes and the standard engine values.

Over an OBDLink, fault codes, coding and the gearbox presets work, but every read is slower than over ENET, and the Tuning presets read the standard OBD channels instead of the engine's full set. Keep the car on a battery charger for coding and service jobs: a module that restarts partway through a write means low voltage.

Hook up the ENET cable

Five steps, a couple of minutes the first time. Ethernet tethering is the one people miss.

  1. Plug it in. The ENET cable into the car's OBD port and into the USB-C Ethernet adapter, and the adapter into the phone. If Android asks which app to open, dismiss it.
  2. Ignition on. The engine can stay off. The adapter's link light comes on once the car's Ethernet wakes up.
  3. Turn on Ethernet tethering. On a Pixel it's Settings, Network & internet, Hotspot & tethering, Ethernet tethering. The Ethernet setup button in the app's ENET dialog opens the same page. The car doesn't hand out network addresses, so the phone has to. The switch stays grey until the cable has a link, so a grey switch means check the cable and the ignition, not the settings.
  4. Connect in the app. Tap Connect at the top of F5X Code, choose ENET cable / USB-C, and tap Connect. The app finds the car on its own; the car's IP address is under Advanced if you ever need it.
  5. Check it answers. Open the Tools tab and tap Check all fault codes. If anything fails, tap Send report: it has every connection step in it.

The red MHD adapter instead

  1. Plug it into the OBD port and switch the ignition on.
  2. Join its Wi-Fi in Android's Wi-Fi settings.
  3. Connect in the app. Tap Connect, choose MHD Wi-Fi / ENET, leave the address at 192.168.4.1, and tap Connect.

What it can and can't do

It reads, logs, codes and runs service jobs on the $45 unlock, and flashes the engine, transmission and steering as paid add-ons.

it can
  • Read and clear fault codes on every module, in English.
  • Log the engine, the six-speed automatic and the power steering, and graph it.
  • Read the oil level from the engine's own sensor.
  • Back up, change and restore coding on eleven modules, with a signature check and read-back on every save.
  • Run fourteen service jobs, each after checking the module fitted to your car.
  • Flash the engine, transmission and power steering as paid add-ons, each checking your module, software and file before it writes.
it can't
  • Change a module's software version. Flashing keeps the software your car runs and writes BMW's data for it, or the tested GP3 steering profile. It isn't a version swap.
  • Change coding on module software it hasn't been checked against. Those modules can still be read and backed up. More are added with each update.
  • Promise a listed option works on your car. MINI changed these modules many times over the years, and there are far more options than on the older cars. As with NCS Expert, an option showing up doesn't mean your car will accept it.
  • Code the newer MINIs (F65, F66, F67, U25, J01, J05).
  • Use a K+DCAN USB cable. That's the older BMW cable; on these cars it's ENET or an OBD adapter.
  • Run on iPhone. Android only.

Flashing: JCW GP3 steering on a standard F56

On 19 November the app flashed BMW's John Cooper Works GP3 power steering profile into the steering rack on my 2015 F56, from the phone, over the ENET cable.

A paid add-on. Steering flashing is a one-time $25 unlock, separate from the $45 app. It changed my own 2015 F56; it checks your exact rack, software and file before it writes and keeps the way back to stock, but nothing on this page is a promise it suits your car.

Why the GP3 file fits

The 2020 GP3 runs the same electric steering unit as my F56: the same Nexteer motor and control unit, the same bootloader and the same 8.21 steering software. What differs is BMW's steering data file, which holds the assist and return-to-centre tuning, and the GP3's own steering rack. So the app keeps the car's software and swaps that one file for the GP3's.

Steering software8.21.0, both cars
Bootloader5.11.0, both cars
Nexteer calibration140.40.0, both cars
BMW steering data10.62.0 → 19.62.0 (GP3)

What the app did

  1. Updated the rack to BMW's latest software. My rack ran 8.13. The app saved its steering settings, updated the bootloader, software, calibration and coding to the 8.21 set the GP3 uses, and put the settings back. Only the steering module was programmed.
  2. Checked the GP3 file. It has to be BMW's signed file, the exact one the app knows. Any other file is refused, and so is a rack that isn't on 8.21 yet.
  3. Installed it. The app writes the software, calibration and GP3 data in the same order as BMW's own update, and the steering module checks each one before it accepts it. Then it switches the steering profile coding to the GP3's, signs that on the phone, restarts the module and reads every coding block back.
  4. Kept the way back. Install OEM EPS 8.21.0 puts BMW's standard file back the same way, and the original backup from before the update stays on the phone.

  • 12 to 35 mph: about twice the pull back to centre at 12 to 19 mph and ten times by 34 mph, where the standard file has nearly let go. It keeps pulling up to about 40 mph.

The GP3 launched on the same rack as mine with an earlier file, and BMW retuned both assist and return when it changed the rack in 2020.

Engine calibration — the $25 engine add-on

Under Tools → Flashing, F5X Code backs up the engine's calibration and flashes a calibration onto the engine software the car already runs. My V12.10.1 tune went on this way on 19 November. Before it writes, it checks the VIN, the exact software and the file; after, the engine checks the new data itself, the app reads the whole calibration back and compares it byte for byte, and puts the engine's coding back. A full read-back takes about two minutes over the ENET cable.

It only flashes the engine software your car already runs. It isn't a way to change software versions. The gearbox (TCU) flash is the same idea and the newest of the three; I haven't run it on a car yet.

Coding and more

Oil level, quick codings, track settings and every module's coding, on the same connection as the logger.

Oil level

On the Tools tab. These cars have no dipstick. The app reads the engine's own oil level sensor: the dash's figure, the raw sensor reading in mm, the short and long-term averages, oil temperature and pressure, each with the byte it came from.

The gauge runs from a quart low to half a quart over. Set your own full mark once, idling warm on level ground with the oil at full, and every reading after that shows how far off full you are. The dash's figure reads Measuring for a while after an oil change; the raw reading doesn't wait.

Quick codings

At the top of the Coding tab: the usual changes as one-tap buttons, each one shown on or off from your latest backup. A tap opens the module's review: it backs the module up, shows what will change, asks before writing, then reads it back. Turn off puts the setting back.

  • Instrument cluster: true speed, digital speed readout, chime in reverse, auto start/stop status.
  • Body module: auto start/stop remembers its setting, or starts every drive off; radio stays on when the door opens; hazards flash under hard braking.
  • Head unit: skip the legal notices, tyre pressures and temperatures.

True speed takes the speedometer's built-in over-read out of the digital readout. The needle keeps its lead. Each quick coding is tied to the exact coding file it was built from, so on a module running other software the app says it doesn't apply and leaves it alone. Each one also says whether it's been tried on a car yet.

Track settings

BMWs of the E82 and E90 era keep a set of helpers running in the stability control module even with DSC and DTC switched off. On those cars, track drivers code them out with NCS Expert, and paradoxical3's guide on e90post is the usual list. The F56's stability control has the same functions under the same BMW names, and each one is a switch in the app.

  • Electronic diff lock (EDLC): the e-diff. It brakes a spinning front wheel to push drive to the other. With a mechanical limited slip diff it fights the diff, so turn it off. On an open diff it's what stops the inside wheel spinning up, so leave it on.
  • Brake standby: moves the pads to the discs when you lift off the throttle fast, then backs them off if you don't brake in time. On track that reads as a pedal that changes from corner to corner. EVB on the E9x.
  • Brake fade compensation: adds pressure as its estimate of brake temperature climbs, so the pedal changes through a session. HPS on the E9x.
  • Dynamic Brake Control: BMW's brake assist, HBA on the E9x. It treats a hard stop as an emergency and builds pressure up to ABS.
  • Engine power cut for hot brakes: FLR. It cuts power when the module's estimate of disc temperature runs high. There's no sensor behind it, and the estimate assumes factory brakes. It's already off on the car in these screenshots.
  • Performance Control: brakes the inside wheels to help the car turn in, the F56's version of Dynamic Performance Control (FDB).

The guide's Maximum Brake Support (HVV) has no switch on the F56. Each of the others is one change: on the Coding tab, tap Stability control, open its settings, flip the switch, check the review and save to the car. The app backs the module up first and reads it back after, and the original backup puts everything back for the street.

Body module recovery

Back up the body module in any state and put a saved backup back unchanged, for a module left half-written by an interrupted session. On the Backups tab.

Coding covers eleven modules: airbag, engine, transmission, stability control, steering, active sound, body, instrument cluster, head unit, controller and climate. The option lists are big. The body module alone has 3,905 named settings and stability control 457, and MINI changed these modules many times over the years, so an option listed for one software version can be ignored or refused by another. As with NCS Expert, seeing an option doesn't mean your car will take it. Every save is read back, so you know whether it landed.
