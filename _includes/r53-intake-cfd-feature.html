What was compared

Both front pieces sit in the same slot in the radiator support and slip onto the same printed snorkel tube. Everything behind them is identical.

  • My snout. The front piece from my cold air intake tube: a 131 mm slot mouth that funnels into a 66 mm socket, with a snap-on cap. The opening through the cap is 3,816 mm².
  • Factory front piece. The part my snout replaces. Its inlet is a shallow tray that fills the same slot; the air turns sideways through an opening about 24 mm tall into a tube whose bore runs from 1,800 to 2,600 mm².
  • Rear port. The printed airbox leaves the factory partition for you to cut, so the rear port is as big as you make it. Closed means the partition is intact.
  • The rest of the tract. The 61 mm printed tube, the factory lower box and the airbox with a dry cone filter, out to the throttle body.

How it was modelled

  • One piece of air. From still air above the radiator support, through the front piece, tube, lower box, filter and housing, to the throttle body end. About 1.16 million cells per run in OpenFOAM, steady flow, k-omega SST.
  • Airflow. 0.269 kg/s, what a supercharged 1.6 needs for about 240 whp. That is a target, not a dyno sheet.
  • Filter. A porous layer set to 500 Pa at this flow. That figure is assumed, not measured.
  • Factory piece. Rebuilt from my 3D scan of the part. The scan caught 64% of the inside wall; for the rest I took the outside and subtracted 2.8 mm of wall, the thickness measured where both sides were scanned.
  • Vacuum and loss. Vacuum is the static pressure below atmosphere at the throttle body end. Loss is the total pressure given up on the way there. About 4.6 kPa of the vacuum in every case is not a loss: it is the speed of the air itself, leaving through the 61 mm outlet at around 76 m/s.

The factory piece costs about 6 kPa more than my snout, and two tests agree on that: 6.2 kPa in the whole tract and 6.4 kPa on a bench, where each part breathes still air and blows into a straight pipe. Nearly all of it is lost inside the front piece itself.

It is not a bad shape. It is a small one, sized for a stock engine and a quiet cabin. At roughly stock airflow, about 0.15 kg/s, the same bench test would put it near 3 kPa, because loss goes with the square of the flow.

What the rear port does

With the port closed, every bit of air goes through the snout and the 61 mm tube at 60 to 100 m/s. Cut a 3-inch port and 71% of it comes in at the back instead, so the tube only carries about a third of the flow and its losses nearly vanish. The loss in the intake falls by 80%, from 9.75 to 1.94 kPa.

Once the port is cut, the front piece matters much less: it is only carrying a third of the air or less.

What I tried that did not beat it

I also ran reshaped snouts through the same model: a mouth that fills more of the slot, a rounded inside corner, and a bell-shaped lip on the cap. They came out between 10.2 and 11.4 kPa of loss, against 9.75 for the snout as it is. So the snout stays. What does change is the cap: a new one that reaches down to the radiator support and grips the outside of the mouth, which costs no flow in the model. It is not printed yet.

What this does not show

  • No car around it. The air starts as a still box above the radiator support. There is no bonnet, grille or road speed.
  • Speed. Above 200 m/s the model is out of its depth, because it treats air as incompressible. The real factory piece would do worse at this flow, not better.
  • Run-to-run wander. My snout’s runs wander 3 to 5%, so differences under about 0.5 kPa mean nothing. The factory gap is twelve times that.
  • Not horsepower. These are pressures in a simulated intake, not a measurement on the car.
