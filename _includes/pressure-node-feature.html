Pressure CAN node

Six pressure sensors and eight 0–5 V inputs on one board. It reads all fourteen and puts them on the car's CAN bus a hundred times a second, so the data logger records aero, intake and cooling pressures alongside everything else.

6pressure channels: four 10 kPa, two 40 kPa
80–5 V analog inputs
100 HzCAN frames at 500 kbit/s
12 Vswitched supply, or USB on the bench

What it measures

Each sensor has one hose barb and reads the pressure there against the air inside the node's box, below it as well as above. Four read to 10 kPa for aero and cooling; two read to 40 kPa for the vacuum a turbo pulls at its inlet. A drop across a part is two channels subtracted. How to fit them.

Aero

Pressure taps on the splitter, underbody, diffuser or wing: how hard the floor sucks at speed and what each surface sees. Dynamic pressure is about 0.5 kPa at 100 km/h and 1.9 kPa at 200 km/h.

Airbox and turbo inlet

Vacuum before and after the air filter, on the two 40 kPa channels. The difference is the filter's restriction, and the second reading is what the turbo sees. A restrictive filter can pull 5 to 7 kPa at full flow, and a turbo inlet more.

Cowl and radiator

Pressure at the cowl and on both faces of the radiator. The drop across the core shows how much air actually goes through it.

Eight analog inputs

For sensors you already have: oil or fuel pressure senders, a spare MAP sensor, ride-height pots, or a tap on an ECU sensor's signal. Each connector carries a switched 5 V supply. Remove its link when tapping a sensor the ECU already powers.

The board

Everything is surface mount and placed by the board house, the pressure sensors included.

How it works

Power

Switched 12 V comes in through a 1 A fuse and a reverse-battery diode, with a 33 V TVS for load dump. An LM5164 buck makes 5 V. Two ideal diodes choose between that and USB, with the car's side first, so the board runs on the bench from a laptop, stays up when both are plugged in, and the two supplies never feed each other. A 3.3 V LDO runs the logic.

Pressure

All six sensors have the same I2C address, so a TCA9548A switch puts each on its own branch and the ESP32-S3 reads them in turn. A reading only counts once the sensor says its conversion finished, and a sensor that stops answering drops out without stalling the others. ZERO averages 100 readings from every channel and subtracts them. Press it with every port open to the same air before a run.

Analog inputs

Each input goes through a divide-by-five divider (500 kΩ to ground, so it barely loads an ECU signal), an isolation switch that stays open until the firmware is running, a buffer and a 16-bit ADC; AIN1–4 and AIN5–8 have one ADC each, converting side by side. A 12 V short on an input only puts 2.4 V on the switch.

Sensor supply

The 5 V on the analog connectors comes off the buck through a 500 mA current-limited switch. It's only there with the car's power on, never from USB alone, and a fault turns it off.

On the CAN bus

A TCAN1042 transceiver sends five 8-byte frames every 10 ms and a status frame every 100 ms, at 500 kbit/s. The IDs and bitrate are firmware settings. A channel that stops updating, isn't fitted, or reads past its range is flagged invalid in its frame instead of repeating its last value. The 120 Ω terminator is off unless you bridge TERM on the board at the end of the bus.

Fitting it to a car

Each channel is one hose: a tap where you want the pressure, run back to the node. Every sensor reads its hose against the air inside the node's box, so most of the work is deciding what that air is, and the hose runs.

Each reading is the port's pressure minus the box's. A drop across a part takes two channels and a subtraction in the logger: DP5 minus DP6 is what the filter costs. The two 40 kPa channels go on the intake because the turbo pulls harder than any aero tap.

The reference

Sealed box on the static line

The best reference for aero taps, because it's the pressure of the air the car is driving through. Seal the node's box, give it one barb, and run the probe's static line to it; all six sensors then read against static. Mount the probe where the bodywork doesn't disturb it, and leave it in the same place so runs compare.

Open box in the cabin

The easy way: leave the box open in the closed cabin and every channel reads against cabin air. The cabin's pressure moves with speed and with the ventilation, so keep the windows shut and the fan the same every run.

Two channels for a drop

For a drop across something, tap both sides and subtract: before and after the filter, in front of and behind the radiator. The reference cancels out. Each sensor is good to about 30 Pa near room temperature, so a small drop needs both zeroed together.

Bottle in the boot

A sealed box onto a bottle with a pinhole makes a slow-moving reference. It still drifts with temperature and with the cabin, so it's a last resort, zeroed before every run.

Hoses and mounting

Hose

2 mm ID silicone fits the barbs. Route it with no kinks, no low spots where water can sit, and nothing near the exhaust. Label both ends with the sensor number.

Length

A long, thin hose slows the reading down. Length matters and the bore matters far more. For a drop measured on two channels, keep their hoses about the same length, or a quick change shows up as a false difference.

Leaks

Push gently on each tap with a syringe and hold it. The reading should stay put. If it creeps back toward zero, a joint is leaking.

The node

Somewhere dry and away from heat. The cabin is easiest, with the hoses through grommets. For the static reference the box needs a sealed lid, sealed cable entries and the one reference barb; any leak lets cabin air in.

Running a test

  1. Zero first. Car parked, engine off, every tap and the reference at the same still air, then press ZERO. It's lost at power-off, so do it each time.
  2. Same road, both ways. Hold a steady speed over the same stretch in each direction and average the two. That cancels most of the wind.
  3. Divide by dynamic pressure. An aero reading divided by DP1's dynamic pressure is a pressure coefficient. It stays the same as speed changes, so a run at 110 km/h compares with one at 130.
  4. One change at a time. Baseline, change, baseline again. If the two baselines disagree, conditions moved and the change can't be judged.
  5. On track, several laps per setup. Drop the odd laps (traffic, a lift) and average the rest. Aero numbers mean most above about 100 km/h.

What the numbers look like

Speed

Dynamic pressure is about 0.5 kPa at 100 km/h, 1.9 kPa at 200 and 4.2 kPa at 300, rising with the square of speed. Floor and diffuser taps sit below the static line, so they read negative.

Intake

On road-car intakes that have been measured, the filter on its own is usually 250 to 500 Pa. On one car the airbox outlet cost more than the filter did. A turbo inlet pulls harder: my F56 intake model puts the whole tract at about 4 kPa at 355 CFM and 9 kPa at 563.

Cooling

Small. In one road test an undertray moved the difference across an intercooler from about −25 Pa to +75 Pa, and cruise intake temperature fell from 149 to 117 °F. The drop across a core rises with the square of the airflow through it, so it compares setups well but isn't a flow figure on its own.

Cowl

The base of the windscreen is usually called a high-pressure spot. On-road tests have found it often isn't the highest at the front of the car, and sometimes it's low. Measure it before cutting an inlet there.

What race teams do

The same ideas at a much bigger scale. Six channels won't map a car the way a team's scanner does, so use them a few points at a time, around the part you're changing.

Rakes in F1 testing

Every team runs aero rakes at some point in testing: rows of Kiel probes, total-pressure heads that tolerate yaw, on a frame behind the front wheels, around the rear axle and diffuser, or on the engine cover. They range from 50 to 600 mm wide and check the car on track against the wind tunnel and CFD.

Taps by the hundred

The cars carry surface taps about 0.5 mm across, plumbed to scanners that read hundreds of channels at once. The wind-tunnel model has taps in the same places, so the two compare directly.

Taped-on patches

Where drilling isn't allowed, like a historic F1 car, teams tape on aluminium patches under 20 mm wide and about 2 mm tall. One such car ran 256 of them.

Pitot-static

Higher-end race cars carry one in clean air, level and pointing forward, on a ±14 kPa differential sensor. Airspeed minus GPS speed gives the headwind around the lap, and straight-line and coast-down runs give downforce and balance estimates. Air density comes from a weather station in the pits.

Brake ducts

Taps at the inlet, along the duct and near the outlet. Air that goes into the inlet doesn't always reach the brake, and the pressure along the duct shows where it's lost.

Formula Student

Student teams check their CFD with a handful of taps, eight on the low-pressure side of an undertray in one case, and run scanners on the wings, sidepods and floor.
