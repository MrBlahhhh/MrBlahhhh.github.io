Pressure CAN node

Six pressure sensors, eight 0–5 V inputs and a barometer on one board. It reads them all and puts them on the car's CAN bus a hundred times a second, in whatever frame layout your logger wants, so aero, intake and cooling pressures land in the same log as everything else. Eight 0/5 V signal outputs, four PWM and four on/off, can pass decisions on to other electronics.

What it measures

Each sensor has one hose barb and reads the pressure there against the air inside the node's box, below it as well as above. Four read to 10 kPa for aero and cooling; two read to 40 kPa for the vacuum a turbo pulls at its inlet. A barometer in the box reads that air, so every channel is also available as absolute pressure. A drop across a part is two channels subtracted. How to fit them.

Aero

Pressure taps on the splitter, underbody, diffuser or wing: how hard the floor sucks at speed and what each surface sees. Dynamic pressure is about 0.5 kPa at 100 km/h and 1.9 kPa at 200 km/h.

Airbox and turbo inlet

Vacuum before and after the air filter, on the two 40 kPa channels. The difference is the filter's restriction, and the second reading is what the turbo sees. A restrictive filter can pull 5 to 7 kPa at full flow, and a turbo inlet more.

Cowl and radiator

Pressure at the cowl and on both faces of the radiator. The drop across the core shows how much air actually goes through it.

Eight analog inputs

For sensors you already have: oil or fuel pressure senders, a spare MAP sensor, ride-height pots, a thermistor, or a tap on an ECU sensor's signal. Each connector carries the board's 5.000 V sensor supply; when tapping a sensor the ECU already powers, remove that connector's 0 Ω link. A thermocouple needs an external K-type amplifier with a 0–5 V output.

Barometer and box temperature

A BMP388 reads the absolute pressure and temperature of the air in the box. That air is the reference every sensor reads against, so you can see whether it moved during a run, and the node can send each channel as absolute pressure as well as gauge.

Supply and battery

The node measures its own sensor supply and the car's 12 V and sends both. A sensor reading that looks wrong can be checked against the supply that fed it, and a short on a sensor's supply wire shows up as a fault instead of a strange number.

The board

Everything is surface mount and placed by the board house, the pressure sensors and the barometer included.

How it works

Power

Switched 12 V comes in through a 1 A fuse and a reverse-battery diode, with a 33 V TVS for load dump. An LM5164 buck makes 5 V for the board. Two ideal diodes choose between that and USB, with the car's side first, so the board runs on the bench from a laptop, stays up when both are plugged in, and the two supplies never feed each other. A 1 A LDO makes 3.3 V for the logic and the radio. The buck's budget is about 0.49 A of its 1 A rating, 0.74 A with a screen plugged in.

Sensor supply

The 5 V on the analog connectors has its own regulator, a TLE4252D tracker fed from the protected 12 V, not from the board's 5 V. It follows a 5.000 V reference, so sensors that scale with their supply see 5.000 V, ±0.3 % typical. Its output survives a short to ground or to battery: a chafed sensor wire can't reset the node or put 12 V on the board. It limits at 250–500 mA, but heat sets the real budget: about 120 mA continuous shared by all eight connectors (117 mA at 14.4 V in a 140 °F (60 °C) box). That's about 15 mA a connector, enough for typical 0.5–4.5 V sensors and pots. Past it the regulator throttles itself until it cools; nothing is damaged. It stays off on USB alone.

Supply sense

The ESP32 measures the sensor supply and the protected 12 V on its own ADC, through dividers that turn 16 V into 2.6 V. The sensor supply counts as faulty outside 4.75–5.25 V: low means an overload or a short to ground, high means a short to battery. Both readings go out on CAN, and the fault sets a flag.

Pressure

All six sensors have the same I²C address, so a TCA9548A switch puts each on its own branch and the ESP32-S3 reads them in turn. A reading only counts once the sensor says its conversion finished, and a sensor that stops answering drops out without stalling the others. ZERO averages 100 readings from every channel and subtracts them. Press it with every port open to the same air before a run; the app and an optional CAN command do the same.

Barometer

A Bosch BMP388 sits on the same I²C bus, ahead of the switch, away from the regulators. It reads the box air's absolute pressure and temperature 25 times a second. When a frame asks for a channel's absolute pressure, the node adds the barometer to that channel's gauge reading.

Analog inputs

Each input goes through a divide-by-five divider (1 MΩ to ground, so it barely loads an ECU signal: about 0.25 % at most on a typical thermistor circuit), a 210 Hz filter, an isolation switch that stays open until the firmware is running, a buffer and a 16-bit ADC. AIN1–4 and AIN5–8 have one ADC each, converting side by side. A 16 V short on an input only puts 3.2 V on the switch.

Thermistors and thermocouples

There are no pull-ups on the board, so every input stays general-purpose. For a thermistor of your own, put a pull-up from the connector's 5 V pin to its signal pin in the sensor's plug, then set the channel up in the app by Beta or by a table; the node sends °C and the app shows °F. To share an ECU's thermistor, connect signal and ground only. A K-type thermocouple needs an external amplifier with a 0–5 V output.

Signal outputs

Four PWM outputs on one connector and four on/off outputs on another, each a 0/5 V push-pull signal for another unit's input: an ECU input, a pump or fan controller, a dash, a PDM input or a solid-state relay. They aren't load drivers; keep the receiving input above about 2 kΩ. Each ESP32 pin goes through an SN74HCT541 buffer on the board's 5 V logic rail, then 1 kΩ to the connector, with an ESD clamp for each pair. A short to 12 V on an output pushes only about 6.5 mA back into the buffer. They run on USB power too.

Outputs off by default

Every output has two pull-downs. 10 kΩ on the buffer's inputs holds it low while the ESP32 boots or resets, and 100 kΩ at the connector holds it low to the receiver even with the node unpowered. With nothing connected the high level is about 4.95 V. As designed, each output is set by a CAN command, by a rule set in the app (a threshold with hysteresis, or a duty table against any reading), or by hand from the app. The hardware is there; the firmware for the outputs isn't written yet.

Links

USB-C for programming, logs and bench power. Bluetooth LE to the phone app. CAN to the car. The ESP32-S3's antenna hangs past the board edge, clear of copper.

On the CAN bus

A TCAN1042 transceiver puts everything on classic CAN. Out of the box the node sends seven frames from 0x520 at 500 kbit/s. What it sends, where each value sits and how often can all be changed from the phone app, without reflashing.

The default frames

Standard 11-bit IDs, little-endian. Pressure is signed, 1 Pa a count; the inputs are 1 mV a count at the connector. A channel that stops updating, isn't fitted, or reads past its range is sent as an invalid code with its flag set, never as its last value.

0x523100 HzHealth: protocol version, counter, valid masks, flags, uptime
0x524100 HzAIN5–AIN8 in mV
0x52510 HzStatus: fitted, overrange, zeroed and fault masks, bus recoveries
0x52610 HzEnvironment: barometer (2 Pa a count), box temperature (0.01 °C a count), sensor supply and battery in mV

The default pressure fields stop at ±32.7 kPa, so a 40 kPa channel reading past that is sent invalid. A layout with 2 Pa a count, like one of the app's presets, carries their whole range.

Your own layout

If your logger expects something else, lay the frames out to suit it in the app's Setup screen. The app checks the layout, the node checks it again, saves it and starts sending it at once; if the node refuses it, the old layout stays.

The phone app

An Android app talks to the node over Bluetooth LE. It decodes the same frames the node puts on CAN, using the node's own layout, so it works on the bench with no logger attached.

Live

Every pressure in kPa or psi, every input in volts or in its own units, then the barometer, box temperature in °F or °C, sensor supply and battery. A channel the node marks invalid says why: over range, sensor fault, not fitted, or no fresh reading. Banners show a sensor-supply fault and a zero in progress.

Graph

Pick channels and watch the last 10, 30 or 60 seconds, with pressures and inputs on separate plots.

Zero and record

Zero the pressures from the phone, after a confirm. Record every sample, 100 a second, to a CSV file in Downloads. Invalid readings are empty cells, never zero.

Setup

The CAN layout, the conversions and the presets, edited as a draft and written to the node in one go. A bit grid shows where each signal sits in its frame. Export the layout as a DBC for the logger, or as a file to load into another node.

An optional screen

A Waveshare ESP32-S3 touch screen plugs into the node's USB-C port. One cable carries power and data: the node is the USB host and powers the screen from its 5 V (about 4.65 V at the screen) whenever it has 12 V. It has five pages: the six pressures with a centre-zero bar against each sensor's range, the eight inputs in volts, a 20-second graph, a zero button, and display settings. The 2.1-inch round LCD is the first choice; there are builds for the 2.8-inch round LCD and two AMOLEDs too. It needs the node's screen firmware, which moves the node's serial console to test pads TP19 and TP20.

Fitting it to a car

Each channel is one hose: a tap where you want the pressure, run back to the node. Every sensor reads its hose against the air inside the node's box, so most of the work is deciding what that air is, and the hose runs. The barometer in the box measures that air, so whichever reference you choose, the log shows what it did.

Each reading is the port's pressure minus the box's; add the barometer's reading for the port's absolute pressure, which the node can also send directly. A drop across a part takes two channels and a subtraction in the logger: DP5 minus DP6 is what the filter costs. The two 40 kPa channels go on the intake because the turbo pulls harder than any aero tap.

The reference

Sealed box on the static line

The best reference for aero taps, because it's the pressure of the air the car is driving through. Seal the node's box, give it one barb, and run the probe's static line to it; all six sensors then read against static. Mount the probe where the bodywork doesn't disturb it, and leave it in the same place so runs compare. The barometer then reads static pressure itself, in absolute terms.

Open box in the cabin

The easy way: leave the box open in the closed cabin and every channel reads against cabin air. The cabin's pressure moves with speed and with the ventilation, so keep the windows shut and the fan the same every run. The barometer logs that air, so you can see how far it moved.

Two channels for a drop

For a drop across something, tap both sides and subtract: before and after the filter, in front of and behind the radiator. The reference cancels out. Each sensor is good to about 30 Pa near room temperature, so a small drop needs both zeroed together.

Bottle in the boot

A sealed box onto a bottle with a pinhole makes a slow-moving reference. It still drifts with temperature and with the cabin, so it's a last resort, zeroed before every run; the barometer shows how much it drifted.

Hoses and mounting

Hose

2 mm ID silicone fits the barbs. Route it with no kinks, no low spots where water can sit, and nothing near the exhaust. Label both ends with the sensor number.

Length

A long, thin hose slows the reading down. Length matters and the bore matters far more. For a drop measured on two channels, keep their hoses about the same length, or a quick change shows up as a false difference.

Leaks

Push gently on each tap with a syringe and hold it. The reading should stay put. If it creeps back toward zero, a joint is leaking.

The node

Somewhere dry and away from heat: the sensor supply's budget assumes the box stays below about 140 °F (60 °C). The cabin is easiest, with the hoses through grommets. For the static reference the box needs a sealed lid, sealed cable entries and the one reference barb; any leak lets cabin air in.

Running a test

  1. Zero first. Car parked, engine off, every tap and the reference at the same still air, then press ZERO, or zero from the app. It's lost at power-off, so do it each time.
  2. Same road, both ways. Hold a steady speed over the same stretch in each direction and average the two. That cancels most of the wind.
  3. Divide by dynamic pressure. An aero reading divided by DP1's dynamic pressure is a pressure coefficient. It stays the same as speed changes, so a run at 110 km/h compares with one at 130.
  4. One change at a time. Baseline, change, baseline again. If the two baselines disagree, conditions moved and the change can't be judged.
  5. On track, several laps per setup. Drop the odd laps (traffic, a lift) and average the rest. Aero numbers mean most above about 100 km/h.

What the numbers look like

Speed

Dynamic pressure is about 0.5 kPa at 100 km/h, 1.9 kPa at 200 and 4.2 kPa at 300, rising with the square of speed. Floor and diffuser taps sit below the static line, so they read negative.

Intake

On road-car intakes that have been measured, the filter on its own is usually 250 to 500 Pa. On one car the airbox outlet cost more than the filter did. A turbo inlet pulls harder: my F56 intake model puts the whole tract at about 4 kPa at 355 CFM and 9 kPa at 563.

Cooling

Small. In one road test an undertray moved the difference across an intercooler from about −25 Pa to +75 Pa, and cruise intake temperature fell from 149 to 117 °F. The drop across a core rises with the square of the airflow through it, so it compares setups well but isn't a flow figure on its own.

Cowl

The base of the windscreen is usually called a high-pressure spot. On-road tests have found it often isn't the highest at the front of the car, and sometimes it's low. Measure it before cutting an inlet there.

What race teams do

The same ideas at a much bigger scale. Six channels won't map a car the way a team's scanner does, so use them a few points at a time, around the part you're changing.

Rakes in F1 testing

Every team runs aero rakes at some point in testing: rows of Kiel probes, total-pressure heads that tolerate yaw, on a frame behind the front wheels, around the rear axle and diffuser, or on the engine cover. They range from 50 to 600 mm wide and check the car on track against the wind tunnel and CFD.

Taps by the hundred

The cars carry surface taps about 0.5 mm across, plumbed to scanners that read hundreds of channels at once. The wind-tunnel model has taps in the same places, so the two compare directly.

Taped-on patches

Where drilling isn't allowed, like a historic F1 car, teams tape on aluminium patches under 20 mm wide and about 2 mm tall. One such car ran 256 of them.

Pitot-static

Higher-end race cars carry one in clean air, level and pointing forward, on a ±14 kPa differential sensor. Airspeed minus GPS speed gives the headwind around the lap, and straight-line and coast-down runs give downforce and balance estimates. Air density comes from a weather station in the pits.

Brake ducts

Taps at the inlet, along the duct and near the outlet. Air that goes into the inlet doesn't always reach the brake, and the pressure along the duct shows where it's lost.

Formula Student

Student teams check their CFD with a handful of taps, eight on the low-pressure side of an undertray in one case, and run scanners on the wings, sidepods and floor.
